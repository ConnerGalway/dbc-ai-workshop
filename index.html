What we're not talking about

You'd already had an AI session in June. So we started with what goes wrong, and why — because the failures share a cause, and the cause is the thesis.

  • AI-written public content

    The problem isn't that it's badly written. It's that it's frictionless and forgettable. Destination BC's own line is the right one: don't let it write the story, write your story.

  • Chatbot recommendations

    A chatbot is a promise that you'll be accountable for whatever it says at 2 a.m. Most organizations make that promise without realizing it.

  • Generated imagery

    It can't clear the rights chain — third-party usage rights, Model Consent Release forms, FOIPPA — so it can't go in the BC Content Hub. It also draws public backlash faster than almost anything else a tourism brand can do.

  • Fabricated trail, route and condition information

    The uncomfortable one for this room. Guide outfitting, backcountry lodges, boating and fishing all operate in country where wrong information is a rescue call.

In every one of these, someone used AI to replace judgment at exactly the moment judgment was the whole job.

Level 3, in practice

The build

One of you sent a real problem: years of research, campaign results, plans and partner records across formats, and no fast way to ask a question across all of it. We built the answer in the room.

About two hours of setup happened before the session. The five minutes you watched was the payoff. That ratio is the lesson — and it's why we showed the scaffolding first: the project, its instructions, the files it was given and what each piece was doing.

The artifact is disposable. The setup pattern is the thing to steal.

The setup pattern

  1. Pick one recurring question

    Not "all our knowledge." The build answered one question well: what do we already know that's relevant to the thing we're about to plan? Projects that try to hold everything answer nothing well.

  2. Gather 10 to 25 files

    What a new hire would need to get up to speed on that question. Research, results, post-mortems, the current plan, the partner list, the asset inventory. Mixed formats are fine. Mixed vintages are fine if you tell it which supersedes which.

  3. Fix the file names, not the shared drive

    The cheapest quality improvement available. Copy the files out, rename the copies with document type and year, upload those. Do not reorganize the drive first; that project never finishes.

  4. Write the instructions

    The files alone give you a search engine. The instructions make it behave like a colleague who knows your organization: who it is, what it holds, what outranks what, cite the file for every claim, flag conflicts, say what's missing. This is the part people skip.

  5. Test it on three things you already know

    Check the citations. If it's right on those, start trusting it on the ones you can't answer. If it's wrong, the fix is almost always a missing file or a missing instruction, not a different tool.

The instruction template from the build, generalized for your organization

Paste this into a Claude or ChatGPT Project's custom instructions and replace the bracketed parts. Half of it will apply to you unchanged.

You are a planning assistant for [ORGANIZATION], a [one-line description]. You are working with the [TEAM] on [the recurring question this project answers].

Your job is to help the team use knowledge the organization already has. Most of the time the answer to their question exists somewhere in the project knowledge. Find it, connect it, and show them where it came from.

What's in the project knowledge
- [Research: name the studies and years]
- [Results: what reports, what period]
- [Debriefs and post-mortems]
- [Plans, partner records, asset lists, board or committee notes]

How to answer
1. Search the project knowledge before answering. If you're drawing on general knowledge rather than our material, say so.
2. Cite the source file for every factual claim — file name and the section, sheet or page.
3. Connect across files. A useful answer usually pulls research, results and assets together.
4. Flag conflicts rather than resolving them silently. Name both sources, say which is more recent, and say which you used.
5. Say what is missing. "We have never tested this" is a useful answer.
6. Separate evidence from inference.

Source hierarchy
- [Newer study] supersedes [older study] wherever they disagree.
- What actually happened (debriefs) outranks what was intended (plans).
- [Any draft records] may describe decisions that have since changed.

Working defaults
- Canadian spelling and CAD. Current fiscal year is [FY].
- Our headline measures are [KPIs].
- Our audience segments are [names].
- Be concise. Lead with the answer, then the evidence.

What not to do
- Do not invent statistics, quotes or source documents.
- Do not treat superseded material as current guidance.
- Do not soften a finding to make past decisions look better.

Before you upload anything real: decide what is and isn't allowed in (personal information, confidential partner data, anything under NDA), who can change the instructions, and who owns the review. If your organization doesn't have an AI policy yet, this is a reasonable forcing function to write one. Test the idea first with files that are already public.

A prompt framework: RCTF

Four parts. The context block is where the work is, and it's the part people skip.

R

Role

Who it should be for this task. A strategist, an editor, an accessibility reviewer.

C

Context

Who you are, who it's for, what's happened already, what's at stake, what you don't want.

T

Task

What to do, specifically. Including what to check, flag, or leave out.

F

Format

Length, structure, sections, citations. And permission to say "that isn't in the files."

Role

You're a senior campaign strategist who knows our organization's research and campaign history.

Context

We're planning the third year of our fall campaign for families within a four-hour drive, September to October travel window, $180,000 approved. I'm presenting a draft brief to my director Thursday and she'll want to know what we're carrying forward from the last two years and what we're changing. I don't want to repeat last spring's mistakes.

Task

Draft the campaign brief. Pull the audience insight from our research, the timing and format decisions from what our campaigns have actually delivered, and the reusable assets and partners from our inventory and partner log. Flag any deadline or rights constraint that affects whether this plan is even possible.

Format

One page. Sections: objective, audience insight, message frame, channel approach, flight timing, assets to reuse, partners, risks. Cite the source file for every factual claim. Mark anything you're inferring. If something isn't in the files, say so.

In a project with good instructions, the Role and half the Format block are already standing policy. That's the argument for spending the afternoon on setup: you write those rules once instead of retyping them forever.

Starter prompts

Nine prompts built for sector associations, tagged by level. Open one, replace the highlighted parts with your own context, copy it into Claude or ChatGPT. None of them writes your public-facing story.

Answer a member's question from your own reports

Level 1

The half-hour hunt through three years of campaign reports, replaced by a question. Works best inside a Project where the reports are already loaded.

You're a research assistant for [ORGANIZATION], a [sector] association in BC. A member has asked: "[the question, e.g. which of our campaigns ran in the Kootenays last year and what did they deliver?"]

Search the attached reports and answer the question directly. For every fact, name the report and page or section it came from. If the reports disagree, say so and tell me which is more recent. If part of the question isn't answered anywhere in the reports, say that plainly rather than guessing.

Format: a short answer I could paste into an email, then a bulleted list of sources.

Structure a Co-op application answer from your own notes

Level 1

Reviewers can see AI-written answers. Use it for structure and consistency, and keep the words yours.

You're an editor helping me prepare a funding application for [PROGRAM]. I've pasted my rough notes below for the question: "[the application question, verbatim]".

Do not rewrite my notes in your own words. Instead: (1) organize them into a logical order that answers the question, (2) point out anything the question asks for that my notes don't cover, (3) flag any claim I make that I haven't supported with a number or an example, and (4) check that my notes are consistent with the goals I've described in the [attached marketing plan / previous application].

Format: my own sentences reordered under short headings, followed by a list of gaps and inconsistencies. Keep my voice exactly as it is.

Write alt text for a batch of photos

Level 1

Accessibility work that almost nobody has time for, done properly in one pass. Attach the images.

You're an accessibility specialist writing alt text for a tourism website. The images attached are from [ORGANIZATION] and will appear on [the page or campaign].

For each image, write alt text that describes what a sighted visitor would take from it — the place, the activity, the mood — in under 125 characters. Don't start with "image of" or "photo of." Don't include marketing language. If the image includes text, transcribe it. If an image is purely decorative, say "decorative" instead of writing alt text.

Format: a two-column list, file name and alt text, in the order the images were attached.

Audit a page for how an AI would read it

Level 2

The first step in GEO is finding out which traveller questions your site doesn't answer. Paste the page text, or connect the URL if your tool can fetch it.

You're a search specialist who understands how AI assistants assemble travel recommendations. Below is the text of a page from [ORGANIZATION]'s website about [topic].

First, list the ten questions a traveller planning a [sector] trip in BC would most likely ask an AI about this topic. Then, for each question, say whether this page answers it clearly, answers it partially, or doesn't answer it. Finally, rewrite the three weakest sections so they answer the question plainly, describing what a visitor does rather than what we offer — but mark these as drafts for me to edit, not final copy.

Format: the question list with a one-word verdict each, then the three draft rewrites.

The $800 pixel. Describe what you can see, and let it walk you through it one step at a time.

You're a patient web developer helping a non-technical person make a change to their own website. The site runs on [WordPress / Squarespace / Wix / other] and I have [admin / editor] access. I want to [describe the change, e.g. add a Meta Pixel to every page / fix a broken link in the footer / add a new FAQ section to the Plan Your Trip page].

Walk me through it one step at a time. After each step, wait for me to tell you what I see on screen before giving the next one. If there's a risk I could break something, tell me before that step and tell me how to undo it. Don't assume I know where any menu is.

Start by asking me what I see when I log in.

Turn an analytics export into a board report

Level 2

Attach the GA4 or campaign export. The report is yours to edit; the analysis is the part that used to take a day.

You're a marketing analyst preparing a report for the board of [ORGANIZATION], a [sector] association. The attached export covers [period] and comes from [GA4 / Meta Ads / our booking platform]. Our goals for this period were [goals, with targets if you have them].

Tell me what happened, in plain language, in this order: the three things that matter most, what changed versus [the previous period / last year], and what I should ask about or act on. Show your numbers. Where the data can't support a conclusion, say so instead of speculating.

Format: one page. Three short headed sections, no more than two charts described in words, and a closing list of three questions for the board.

Translate and check consistency across versions

Level 1

For member communications and visitor information — not public storytelling.

You're a professional translator working for [ORGANIZATION]. Translate the text below from English into [language] for a [member notice / visitor safety page / booking confirmation] audience. Keep place names, organization names and program names in English. Keep the register [formal / plain and friendly].

Then compare your translation against the English and list any place where a meaning could shift, a term has no direct equivalent, or a legal or safety phrase needs a human translator's review before publishing.

Format: the translation, then the review list.

Digest a stack of industry reports monthly

Level 3

The hours a week spent reading to stay current. Attach the reports, or run this in a Project where they're stored, and you have the beginnings of your own industry scan.

You're a research analyst for [ORGANIZATION], a [sector] association in BC. Our members are [describe: e.g. small operators, mostly seasonal, dependent on the drive market]. I've attached [number] industry reports published this month.

For each report, tell me in two sentences whether it matters to our members and why. Then, across all of them, identify the three findings most relevant to [our current priority, e.g. shoulder-season demand / US visitation / booking behaviour], with the report and page for each. Flag anywhere the reports contradict each other. Finish with one paragraph I could send to members as "what we're watching this month" — marked as a draft for me to edit.

Format: report-by-report list, then the three findings, then the contradictions, then the draft paragraph.

Set up your first Project

Level 3

The setup pattern, run by the tool itself. Upload your files first, then send this. It writes the draft instructions; you correct them.

I've just created a Project for [ORGANIZATION] and uploaded [number] files. The recurring question I want this Project to answer is: "[e.g. what do we already know that's relevant to the thing we're about to plan?"]

Read the files and do four things: (1) list what's in them, one line per file, with the year each one covers; (2) tell me which files overlap or contradict each other and which is more recent; (3) tell me what's obviously missing for the question I've described; and (4) draft the custom instructions for this Project — who you are, what you hold, what supersedes what, cite every claim, flag conflicts, say what's missing — written so I can paste them straight in.

Format: the four sections in order. Mark anything in the draft instructions you're unsure about so I can correct it.
